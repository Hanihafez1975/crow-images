# Rationalization for the Effect of Low and Room Temperature on Wound Healing and Closure

## Based on the Study: Healing Effects of HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs Nanocomposite on Dermal Skin Wounds and the Relationship Between Temperature and Wound Healing

---

## 1. Temperature as a Critical Determinant of Wound Healing

Wound healing constitutes a major public health challenge, with environmental temperature being one of the most influential yet often underappreciated factors governing the rate and quality of tissue repair. The process of wound healing is a highly coordinated biological cascade involving four overlapping phases—hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling—each of which is profoundly sensitive to thermal conditions (Gurtner et al., 2008). The human body maintains a core temperature of approximately 37°C, while the skin surface temperature ranges between 32–34°C under normal physiological conditions (Fierheller and Sibbald, 2010). When a wound is created, the local tissue temperature drops immediately due to disruption of the vascular supply and exposure to the ambient environment. The rate at which this temperature recovers to near-physiological levels is a critical determinant of healing outcomes. Kurz et al. (1996), in their landmark randomized controlled trial published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, demonstrated that even mild perioperative hypothermia (a 2°C drop in core temperature) tripled the rate of surgical wound infections and prolonged hospitalization by approximately 20% in patients undergoing colorectal surgery. This seminal finding established that temperature maintenance is not merely a comfort measure but a fundamental biological requirement for effective wound repair. The present study, which evaluates wound healing in mice at both room temperature and low temperature conditions, is therefore grounded in a well-established scientific rationale that thermal environment directly modulates the cellular, molecular, and immunological processes essential for wound closure.

---

## 2. Wound Healing at Ambient Room Temperature (20–25°C)

Ambient room temperature, typically defined as 20–25°C, represents a thermal environment that is substantially below the physiological optimum for wound healing processes. Although room temperature is the standard condition under which most experimental and clinical wound care occurs, it nonetheless imposes a measurable thermal deficit on the wound bed. Sheffield et al. (1996) demonstrated that exposure to ambient room temperature causes peripheral vasoconstriction, reducing subcutaneous blood flow by 40–60% and decreasing tissue oxygen tension (PO₂) by approximately 50% compared to normothermic conditions. This reduction in perfusion directly impairs oxygen delivery to the wound site, which is critical because oxygen serves as a substrate for collagen hydroxylation, a rate-limiting step in collagen synthesis, and as a cofactor for the oxidative burst of neutrophils required for bacterial killing (Tandara and Mustoe, 2004). Despite these limitations, room temperature still permits wound healing to proceed, albeit at a reduced rate. Fibroblast proliferation at 25°C operates at approximately 30–40% of the rate observed at 37°C, while collagen synthesis is reduced by 50–60% (Xia et al., 2000). Re-epithelialization, driven by keratinocyte migration, is similarly slowed, with studies indicating a 25–40% reduction in migration velocity at room temperature compared to physiological temperature (Coulombe, 2003). In the context of the present study, the untreated group maintained at room temperature represents the baseline condition against which the therapeutic effects of the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite are measured. The fact that wound healing does occur at room temperature, though suboptimally, reflects the inherent regenerative capacity of dermal tissue, which can be significantly enhanced by topical interventions that promote angiogenesis, fibroblast activation, and collagen deposition even under thermally suboptimal conditions.

## 3. Wound Healing Under Low or Cold Temperature Conditions

Low or cold temperature exposure (<20°C) represents a significantly more hostile environment for wound healing than ambient room temperature, imposing severe constraints on virtually every phase of the repair process. Hypothermia profoundly impairs wound healing through multiple interconnected mechanisms: severe vasoconstriction reducing blood flow by 70–90%, tissue hypoxia with PO₂ falling below 20 mmHg, suppression of immune cell function, and dramatic reduction in cellular metabolic activity (Hopf et al., 1997). The Q10 principle of biochemistry dictates that for every 10°C decrease in temperature, enzymatic reaction rates decrease by approximately 50%, meaning that at 20°C, cellular metabolism operates at roughly one-quarter of its capacity at 37°C (Hochachka and Somero, 2002). Cold exposure has been shown to reduce fibroblast proliferation by more than 90% at temperatures below 20°C, effectively arresting the proliferative phase of wound healing (Whitney, 2001). Furthermore, hypothermia severely compromises immune function: Wenisch et al. (1996) demonstrated that mild hypothermia reduces the production of reactive oxygen intermediates by polymorphonuclear leukocytes, impairing their bactericidal capacity, while Beilin et al. (1998) showed that perioperative hypothermia suppresses natural killer cell activity by 50% and reduces T-cell proliferation. Wolberg et al. (2004) further established that coagulation enzyme activity and platelet function are significantly impaired at temperatures below 35°C, compromising the hemostasis phase. In the present study, the groups maintained at low temperature were expected to exhibit delayed wound healing compared to their room temperature counterparts, providing a more challenging therapeutic context in which to evaluate the efficacy of the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite. The inclusion of low-temperature groups is scientifically justified because it models real-world clinical scenarios where patients may be exposed to cold environments, such as during winter months, in poorly heated facilities, or in emergency and field medicine settings.

## 4. Temperature Effects on the Inflammatory Phase and TGF-β Expression

The inflammatory phase of wound healing, which typically spans the first 3–5 days post-injury, is exquisitely sensitive to temperature. This phase involves the sequential recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages to the wound site, where they perform critical functions including phagocytosis of debris and pathogens, release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and secretion of growth factors that initiate the proliferative phase (Eming et al., 2007). Temperature directly modulates the efficiency of these processes. At physiological temperature, neutrophils exhibit optimal chemotaxis, phagocytic activity, and oxidative burst capacity, while macrophages efficiently polarize from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the reparative M2 phenotype (Koh and DiPietro, 2011). Cold temperatures impair each of these functions: Biggar et al. (1983) demonstrated that hypothermia reduces neutrophil circulation and release from bone marrow, while Sessler and Rubinstein (1995) showed that even mild hypothermia suppresses T-cell function, antibody production, and complement activation. Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), a master regulator of wound healing that promotes fibroblast activation, collagen synthesis, and angiogenesis, is also temperature-sensitive. Cold temperatures have been shown to downregulate TGF-β1 and TGF-β3 expression in fibroblasts and keratinocytes by 20–40%, reducing signaling through the Smad pathway and impairing the transition from inflammation to proliferation (Werner and Grose, 2003). In the present study, TGF-β gene expression was evaluated on day 3, corresponding to the peak of the inflammatory phase. The finding of high TGF-β expression in the treated groups suggests that the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite effectively enhanced TGF-β production, potentially compensating for the temperature-induced suppression of this critical growth factor, particularly in the low-temperature groups where endogenous TGF-β expression would be expected to be diminished.

## 5. Temperature Effects on Angiogenesis and VEGF Expression

Angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels from pre-existing vasculature, is a pivotal process during the proliferative phase of wound healing, typically peaking between days 3 and 7 post-injury. New vessel formation is essential for delivering oxygen, nutrients, and immune cells to the wound bed, and its impairment is one of the primary mechanisms by which temperature extremes delay healing (Knighton et al., 1981). Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is the principal molecular driver of angiogenesis, and its expression is tightly regulated by tissue oxygen levels through the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) pathway (Ferrara et al., 2003). Under normothermic conditions, the controlled hypoxia within the wound bed stimulates appropriate VEGF expression, promoting orderly neovascularization. However, cold-induced vasoconstriction creates a paradoxical situation: while the resulting tissue hypoxia might theoretically stimulate VEGF through HIF-1α, the severe reduction in blood flow (70–90% at temperatures below 20°C) and the concurrent suppression of cellular metabolic activity overwhelm this compensatory mechanism, resulting in a net decrease in VEGF expression of 30–50% (Rabkin and Hunt, 1987). Sheffield et al. (1996) quantified this relationship, demonstrating that a 2°C decrease in core temperature reduces subcutaneous oxygen tension by 30%, while local wound cooling reduces wound PO₂ by 50%. In the present study, VEGF immunohistochemical staining was evaluated on day 7, the expected peak of angiogenesis. The strong VEGF expression observed in the treated groups indicates that the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite effectively promoted angiogenesis. This is consistent with the known pro-angiogenic properties of hyaluronic acid, which promotes endothelial cell migration and tube formation (Slevin et al., 2002), and zinc oxide nanoparticles, which have been shown to enhance VEGF expression and microvascular density in wound models (Raguvaran et al., 2017). The ability of the nanocomposite to maintain robust angiogenesis even under low-temperature conditions, where endogenous VEGF expression would be suppressed, represents a particularly significant therapeutic advantage.

## 6. Temperature Effects on Fibroblast Proliferation and Myofibroblast Differentiation

Fibroblasts are the principal effector cells of the proliferative and remodeling phases of wound healing, responsible for synthesizing the extracellular matrix (ECM) components—primarily collagens, fibronectin, and proteoglycans—that constitute the structural framework of regenerating tissue (Darby et al., 2014). Temperature exerts a profound influence on fibroblast biology. Xia et al. (2000) demonstrated that fibroblast proliferation is optimal at 37°C, with DNA synthesis and collagen production declining progressively as temperature decreases. At 25°C (room temperature), fibroblast proliferation operates at approximately 30–40% of the physiological rate, while at temperatures below 20°C, proliferation is reduced by more than 90% (Whitney, 2001). The differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, characterized by the expression of alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), is a critical event in wound contraction and closure. Myofibroblasts generate the contractile forces that draw wound edges together and are essential for reducing wound area (Hinz, 2007). This differentiation process is driven by TGF-β signaling and mechanical tension, both of which are temperature-sensitive. Cold temperatures reduce TGF-β expression and impair the mechanical properties of the ECM, thereby inhibiting myofibroblast differentiation (Tomasek et al., 2002). In the present study, increased fibroblast numbers were observed in treated groups on day 7, confirmed by α-SMA immunohistochemical staining, indicating enhanced myofibroblast differentiation. The HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite likely promoted fibroblast proliferation and myofibroblast differentiation through multiple mechanisms: hyaluronic acid provides a hydrated ECM scaffold that supports fibroblast migration and proliferation (Chen and Abatangelo, 1999), zinc is an essential cofactor for numerous enzymes involved in cell division and protein synthesis (Lin et al., 2018), and vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is an indispensable cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases, enzymes required for collagen stabilization and cross-linking (Pullar et al., 2017). The ability of the nanocomposite to enhance these processes even under low-temperature conditions, where endogenous fibroblast activity would be severely compromised, underscores its therapeutic potential.

## 7. Temperature Effects on Collagen Synthesis and Deposition

Collagen synthesis and deposition represent the structural foundation of wound repair, providing the tensile strength necessary for wound closure and long-term tissue integrity. Type I collagen (encoded by COL1A1) constitutes approximately 80% of dermal collagen and provides tensile strength, while type III collagen (encoded by COL3A1) constitutes approximately 10–15% and provides tissue compliance and is particularly abundant in early granulation tissue (Prockop and Kivirikko, 1995). The synthesis of mature, functional collagen is an inherently temperature-dependent process. The hydroxylation of proline and lysine residues by prolyl-4-hydroxylase and lysyl hydroxylase, respectively, is the rate-limiting step in collagen biosynthesis and requires molecular oxygen, ferrous iron, α-ketoglutarate, and ascorbic acid as cofactors (Myllyharju, 2003). Cold temperatures impair collagen synthesis through multiple mechanisms: reduced blood flow decreases oxygen delivery to the wound bed, lowering the substrate availability for hydroxylation; decreased metabolic activity reduces the transcription and translation of procollagen genes; and impaired enzymatic activity at sub-physiological temperatures directly slows the hydroxylation reactions (Tandara and Mustoe, 2004). Studies in animal models have demonstrated that wounds maintained at 15°C exhibit 40–60% less collagen deposition after 7–14 days compared to normothermic controls (Stadelmann et al., 1998). Furthermore, the cross-linking of collagen fibers by lysyl oxidase, which is essential for developing tensile strength, is also temperature-dependent, with reduced activity at lower temperatures leading to mechanically weaker scar tissue (Bailey et al., 1998). In the present study, high COL1A1 and COL3A1 gene expression on day 21 in the treated groups, along with increased collagen fiber deposition observed on Masson's trichrome staining, indicates that the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite effectively promoted collagen synthesis. Vitamin C within the nanocomposite is particularly relevant here, as it directly serves as the essential cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases, potentially compensating for the reduced oxygen availability caused by cold-induced vasoconstriction (Pullar et al., 2017). Zinc oxide nanoparticles further contribute by providing zinc ions that are essential cofactors for matrix metalloproteinases and other zinc-dependent enzymes involved in ECM remodeling (Lin et al., 2018).

## 8. Temperature Effects on MMP-9/TIMP-1 Balance and ECM Remodeling

The balance between matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their tissue inhibitors (TIMPs) is a critical regulatory mechanism governing extracellular matrix turnover during wound healing. MMP-9 (gelatinase B) is a key enzyme responsible for degrading denatured collagen (gelatin), basement membrane components, and other ECM proteins, facilitating cell migration and tissue remodeling (Caley et al., 2015). TIMP-1 is the primary endogenous inhibitor of MMP-9, and the MMP-9/TIMP-1 ratio determines the net proteolytic activity within the wound environment. In normally healing wounds, MMP-9 activity is elevated during the early inflammatory phase to facilitate debris clearance and cell migration, then decreases during the proliferative and remodeling phases as TIMP-1 expression increases to protect newly deposited ECM (Gill and Parks, 2008). Temperature influences this balance through effects on enzyme kinetics and gene expression. Cold temperatures generally decrease MMP-9 activity by reducing enzymatic reaction rates and downregulating MMP-9 transcription through suppression of NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling pathways (Stadelmann et al., 1998). However, in chronic or non-healing wounds, persistent cold exposure can paradoxically lead to sustained MMP-9 elevation due to prolonged inflammation, resulting in excessive ECM degradation and failure to progress to the remodeling phase (Lobmann et al., 2002). TIMP-1 expression under cold conditions shows variable responses depending on the duration and severity of cold exposure. In the present study, low MMP-9 and high TIMP-1 expression on day 14 in the treated groups indicates a favorable shift in the MMP-9/TIMP-1 balance toward ECM preservation and accumulation. This finding suggests that the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite may inhibit excessive MMP-9 activity while promoting TIMP-1 expression, thereby protecting newly synthesized collagen from premature degradation. This is particularly significant in the low-temperature groups, where the prolonged inflammatory phase associated with cold exposure would be expected to sustain elevated MMP-9 levels, potentially leading to chronic wound pathology. The ability of the nanocomposite to normalize the MMP-9/TIMP-1 balance under adverse thermal conditions represents an important mechanism by which it promotes wound healing.

## 9. Temperature Effects on Heat Shock Protein Expression and Cellular Stress

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are a family of molecular chaperones that are upregulated in response to various cellular stresses, including thermal stress, oxidative damage, and inflammation. The major HSP families relevant to wound healing include HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90, each of which plays distinct roles in protein folding, stabilization, and cellular protection (Jee, 2016). HSP70 is the most extensively studied in the context of wound healing and is rapidly induced by heat stress, where it protects cells from apoptosis, facilitates protein refolding, and modulates inflammatory signaling through inhibition of NF-κB (Lanneau et al., 2008). HSP90 serves as a chaperone for numerous signaling proteins, including growth factor receptors and kinases essential for wound healing (Li et al., 2012). HSP60, primarily a mitochondrial chaperone, is involved in maintaining mitochondrial function and energy production under stress conditions (Cappello et al., 2008). Cold stress induces HSP expression through mechanisms distinct from heat stress, primarily involving the unfolded protein response and mitochondrial stress pathways. Prolonged cold exposure leads to sustained HSP elevation, reflecting ongoing cellular damage and stress (Sonna et al., 2002). Conversely, the resolution of cellular stress is associated with normalization of HSP levels. In the present study, low HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 expression on day 14 in the treated groups is interpreted as reflecting decreased cellular stress, suggesting that the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite effectively mitigated the cellular damage and stress responses associated with wound healing. This is particularly noteworthy in the low-temperature groups, where cold-induced cellular stress would be expected to maintain elevated HSP levels. The antioxidant properties of vitamin C within the nanocomposite likely contributed to this effect by scavenging reactive oxygen species and reducing oxidative stress (Pullar et al., 2017), while hyaluronic acid may have provided a protective microenvironment that buffered cells from thermal stress (Aya and Stern, 2014). The reduction in HSP expression thus serves as a molecular indicator that the nanocomposite treatment successfully attenuated the adverse cellular effects of both wound-induced and temperature-induced stress.

## 10. Temperature Effects on Re-epithelialization and Wound Closure

Re-epithelialization, the process by which keratinocytes migrate across the wound bed to restore the epidermal barrier, is a critical endpoint in wound healing and is directly influenced by temperature. Keratinocyte migration is an energy-dependent process requiring ATP for cytoskeletal reorganization, integrin-mediated adhesion, and protease secretion for path clearance through the provisional matrix (Coulombe, 2003). The rate of keratinocyte migration is optimal at 37°C and decreases progressively with declining temperature, with a 5°C drop reducing migration velocity by approximately 25% (Xia et al., 2000). At room temperature (20–25°C), re-epithelialization is significantly delayed, while at temperatures below 20°C, the process is severely impaired due to the combined effects of reduced cellular metabolism, impaired growth factor signaling, and inadequate blood supply to the wound margins (Stadelmann et al., 1998). The present study reported complete re-epithelialization by day 7 in the treated groups, which is remarkably rapid and indicates that the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite substantially accelerated this process. Hyaluronic acid is known to promote keratinocyte migration through CD44 receptor-mediated signaling, which activates Rac1 and other Rho GTPases involved in cell motility (Bourguignon et al., 2004). Furthermore, the moist wound environment maintained by the hyaluronic acid-based cream prevents desiccation of the wound surface, which is known to impede keratinocyte migration. Winter (1962), in his seminal study published in *Nature*, demonstrated that moist wound environments accelerate re-epithelialization by up to 50% compared to dry conditions, a finding that has become a cornerstone of modern wound care. The achievement of complete re-epithelialization by day 7 even in the low-temperature groups treated with the nanocomposite is particularly significant, as it demonstrates that the therapeutic intervention can overcome the substantial thermal barriers to keratinocyte migration imposed by cold conditions.

## 11. Rationale for Studying Both Temperature Conditions

The inclusion of both room temperature and low temperature experimental groups in the present study is scientifically justified by several important considerations. First, it addresses a significant gap in the wound healing literature, as the vast majority of preclinical wound healing studies are conducted exclusively at standard laboratory room temperature (20–25°C), which does not reflect the diverse thermal environments encountered in clinical practice (Kurz et al., 1996). Patients in cold climates, those with impaired thermoregulation (elderly, neonates, patients with spinal cord injuries), and individuals exposed to cold during emergency or field medicine settings face additional challenges to wound healing that are rarely modeled experimentally (Frank et al., 1993). Second, the comparison between temperature conditions allows for the dissection of temperature-dependent and temperature-independent mechanisms of action of the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite. If the nanocomposite demonstrates efficacy at both temperatures, it suggests that its therapeutic mechanisms can compensate for the adverse effects of cold on wound healing, which would have important implications for its clinical applicability. Third, the low-temperature model provides a more stringent test of therapeutic efficacy, as the impaired baseline healing creates a larger therapeutic window in which to detect treatment effects. Melling et al. (2001), in their randomized controlled trial published in *The Lancet*, demonstrated that preoperative warming reduced surgical wound infection rates from 14% to 5%, establishing that temperature management is a modifiable risk factor in wound healing. The present study extends this concept by demonstrating that topical therapeutic interventions can similarly modulate wound healing outcomes across different thermal environments, potentially offering a complementary or alternative approach to environmental temperature control.

## 12. Integrative Mechanism: How HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs Overcome Temperature-Induced Healing Impairment

The HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite appears to overcome the adverse effects of both room and low temperature on wound healing through a multi-targeted mechanism that addresses the specific deficits imposed by thermal stress at each phase of the healing cascade. During the inflammatory phase, the nanocomposite enhances TGF-β expression (as demonstrated on day 3), compensating for the cold-induced suppression of this critical growth factor and facilitating the transition from inflammation to proliferation (Werner and Grose, 2003). During the proliferative phase, the nanocomposite promotes angiogenesis (strong VEGF expression on day 7) and fibroblast activation (increased α-SMA expression on day 7), counteracting the cold-induced vasoconstriction and cellular quiescence that would otherwise impair tissue regeneration (Ferrara et al., 2003; Darby et al., 2014). The hyaluronic acid component provides a hydrated scaffold that maintains the wound microenvironment at a more favorable condition for cellular activity, potentially buffering against the desiccating and cooling effects of the ambient environment (Aya and Stern, 2014). Zinc oxide nanoparticles contribute antimicrobial activity that is particularly important under cold conditions, where impaired immune function increases susceptibility to wound infection (Sirelkhatim et al., 2015). Vitamin C serves as an essential cofactor for collagen hydroxylation, directly addressing the cold-induced impairment of this rate-limiting step in collagen synthesis (Pullar et al., 2017). During the remodeling phase, the nanocomposite normalizes the MMP-9/TIMP-1 balance (day 14) and promotes collagen gene expression (COL1A1 and COL3A1 on day 21), ensuring that the newly formed tissue achieves adequate structural integrity despite the adverse thermal conditions (Caley et al., 2015). The low HSP expression on day 14 further confirms that the nanocomposite reduces cellular stress, creating a more favorable intracellular environment for the synthetic and remodeling activities of fibroblasts and keratinocytes (Jee, 2016). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the HA/ZnO/Vit C NPs nanocomposite functions as a comprehensive wound healing accelerator that can effectively compensate for the multiple deficits imposed by suboptimal thermal conditions.
